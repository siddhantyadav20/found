# FOUND

# CHAPTER 1 — SHAGUN

**Master Narrative Pass**

A grounded psychological mystery built around a stranger’s phone, a Delhi wedding, a shooting, a cover-up, and competing versions of the same night.

Purpose: establish the strongest story architecture before full dialogue and gameplay scripting.

## 1. Creative North Star

The chapter should feel simple on the surface and increasingly difficult to interpret underneath.

The player begins with a practical problem: a stranger’s phone is now in their hands. They then uncover a death, identify the phone owner, and build a theory around what happened. Each episode breaks a larger assumption rather than simply adding another clue.

The target feeling is not “I found the answer.” It is “I found an answer, and then discovered I had been answering the wrong question.”

Core player experience:

- Discover a stranger through their private digital life.
- Build a theory using incomplete but authentic evidence.
- Feel confident in that theory.
- Encounter evidence that makes the theory incomplete or misleading.
- Reinterpret earlier evidence rather than simply receiving a new answer.
- Reach an ending that depends on what the player actually established and chose to do.

## 2. What This Pass Preserves

The central Shaadi Shot story remains intact. The major change is the depth of interpretation around it.

- Sameer remains the phone owner and a wedding photographer.
- The chapter remains set in Delhi and centers on a wedding/farmhouse incident.
- Dilip remains the young worker who is shot and later dies.
- The body is still deliberately burned as part of the cover-up.
- Sameer remains directly responsible for firing the shot.
- Bhasin remains the person who turns a terrible accident into a controlled cover-up.
- The phone contains both useful evidence and Sameer’s own framing of that evidence.
- Chapter 1 remains a complete story with a sequel hook into Mumbai.

## 3. What Changes

The earlier versions made the final mystery too close to “who pulled the trigger.” This pass makes that only one layer.

- The identity of the shooter is discovered earlier than the final act.
- The central mystery becomes the complete causal chain from shooting to death to cover-up.
- The player discovers that Dilip survives the shooting for a meaningful period.
- The player discovers that a genuine attempt to get him out existed — and was stopped by misinformation.
- Sameer is not a forgotten-memory unreliable narrator. He remembers what he did but constructs a self-protective meaning around it.
- The family possesses evidence against Sameer while Sameer possesses evidence against the family.
- The phone is deliberately curated. It is not a neutral archive and not a magical evidence box.
- The final revelation concerns what Sameer wants the player to conclude, not just what physically happened.

## 4. The Four Versions of the Night

The chapter is built around four overlapping versions of reality. None is wholly false.

| Layer | What it represents | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| The visible record | Photos, videos, messages, payments, locations, calls. | The player has concrete things to inspect. |
| Sameer’s story | His interpretation of what those records mean. | He is both witness and narrator. |
| Other people’s stories | Kunal, Bhasin, Nitin, Raju and Meera each know different pieces. | No single person can explain everything. |
| Objective truth | The actual chronology and causal chain. | This is what the player must reconstruct, not simply receive. |

## 5. Objective Truth — What Actually Happened

This is the fixed reality of the chapter.

1. Sameer is hired to photograph a wealthy Delhi wedding at a farmhouse near Chhattarpur.
2. Kunal Sehgal, the groom’s younger brother, has access to his father’s licensed revolver and is already engaging in celebratory firing.
3. Sameer is also trying to use the wedding to produce a more cinematic reel for his own career. This makes him actively involved in a private shoot rather than merely documenting the crowd.
4. Dilip, a young catering worker who has begun helping Sameer’s crew on small jobs, is asked to assist with the private setup.
5. Kunal gives Sameer the loaded revolver during the private sequence. Sameer fires once for the camera. The second discharge is made while posing and is directed lower than intended.
6. The bullet hits Dilip. He is alive after the impact.
7. Bhasin immediately prioritizes containing the incident and preventing an uncontrolled police/medical response.
8. Dilip is moved to a service area instead of being taken straight to hospital.
9. Sameer is frightened and does attempt to help. He also knows that a proper emergency response exposes him and the family.
10. Nitin, independently and imperfectly, attempts to arrange a vehicle/medical escape for Dilip.
11. Sameer tells Nitin that Dilip has already been taken for treatment. Sameer knows that statement is false, but tells himself that Bhasin has control and that Nitin’s intervention will only make the situation worse.
12. Nitin abandons his attempt. A vehicle reaches the property but leaves without Dilip.
13. Dilip remains on the property and eventually dies.
14. The people around the incident now have a second problem: there is a body whose existence contradicts every attempt to make the night disappear.
15. Bhasin organizes the destruction of the body before dawn. Sameer participates and records a short portion of the fire.
16. The family’s legitimate payment to Sameer becomes leverage for silence and cooperation after the incident.
17. Sameer later discovers evidence that confirms he fired the shot. He cannot honestly frame himself as an innocent witness anymore.
18. At the same time, he also possesses evidence that the family obstructed the aftermath and that a possible intervention was abandoned.
19. Sameer prepares his phone before surrendering/sending it. He deliberately preserves evidence that supports the family’s wrongdoing while omitting or minimizing the evidence of his own false assurance.
20. He sends the phone because he wants an independent reconstruction — but he still wants the final reconstruction to separate “my accident” from “their crime.”

|---|---|---|---|
| Sameer Khurana, 27 | Ambitious wedding photographer. | Protect career, avoid becoming the sole face of the incident, and prove the family’s wrongdoing. | He remembers his own actions. His deception is mainly about what those actions mean and where responsibility should end. |
| Dilip Kumar Mahto, 19 | Catering worker known as “Chhotu.” | Earn money and move toward better work. | He trusts Sameer because the relationship is real; that trust becomes part of how he is placed in danger. |
| Kunal Sehgal, 24 | Wealthy groom’s brother; social and careless. | Keep the wedding and his own reputation intact. | He enables the shooting and later helps contain it, while seeing himself as less responsible because he did not fire the fatal shot. |
| Vinod Bhasin, 58 | Calm former police officer now handling security/liaison work. | Control the incident and preserve his usefulness to the family. | He sees control as problem-solving and keeps postponing the moment when consequences become unavoidable. |
| Nitin, 22 | Sameer’s assistant and junior crew member. | Keep earning, protect himself, and stop being disposable. | He really tries to help, but he also obeys a false reassurance without verifying it. |
| Raju Mahto, 24 | Dilip’s older brother and delivery rider. | Find Dilip and establish what happened. | He wants to protect Dilip but sometimes speaks for him before knowing what Dilip would choose. |

## 7. Mystery Ladder

The player should not simply receive bigger answers. The question itself should evolve.

| Stage | Player question | Working theory | Later complication |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who owns this phone? | A missing photographer sent it for help. | The phone was intentionally prepared. |
| 2 | What happened at the wedding? | Sameer witnessed a killing and the family covered it up. | The player is compressing multiple moments into one story. |
| 3 | Who is Dilip? | An anonymous worker caught in the wrong place. | Sameer knew him personally and owed him a promise. |
| 4 | Who fired the gun? | Kunal is the shooter. | Sameer fired the later shot that hit Dilip. |
| 5 | Did the shot kill Dilip? | Yes; everything after it is cleanup. | Dilip remained alive, creating a long window of decisions. |
| 6 | Was anyone trying to save him? | No; the family controlled everything. | Nitin was trying to create a real route out. |
| 7 | Why did that attempt fail? | Bhasin blocked everything. | Sameer knowingly told Nitin that help had already happened. |
| 8 | Why did Sameer keep evidence? | To expose the family. | Also to construct a version in which his responsibility stops before the death. |
| 9 | Why did he send the phone? | To confess. | To force an independent investigation while still controlling the framing. |
| 10 | What is the real truth? | One person is to blame. | The night is a chain of choices, with different responsibilities at different points. |

## 8. Episode 1 — MISSED CALLS

Objective: discover whose phone this is and why the owner appears to be connected to a death.

End belief: Sameer witnessed a murder and is being hunted.

The episode should feel like entering a stranger’s life before realizing that something terrible happened inside it.

Sequence:

1. The phone arrives with a handwritten note addressed to “M.” The note warns against going to the local police station and references Bhasin.
2. The player identifies Sameer through ordinary content: Instagram, SK Films, invoices, family chats, camera work, financial pressure, and Mumbai correspondence.
3. The player finds Dilip in a wedding photograph. He is smiling and holding a tray. At this stage, he is just another face in the gallery.
4. A missing-person poster for Dilip appears in the phone. A Bihar number keeps asking who now has the handset.
5. Sameer’s unsent voice notes suggest that Kunal was firing, a boy fell, treatment did not happen, and the body was burned.
6. The player finds the short fire recording. It becomes the apparent visual proof that the voice notes are describing a real death.
7. The player discovers Sameer’s ordinary Mumbai future: booking, job contact, travel plans. This complicates the idea that he simply vanished after witnessing a crime.
8. A location/device alert suggests someone is trying to find the phone after it has changed hands.
9. The episode ends with the player still believing the central story is a straightforward murder-and-cover-up, but with one unresolved question: why does Sameer’s phone look like it was prepared before it was sent?

Episode 1 key reveal: The player has identified the victim and the incident, but not the event’s chronology.

## 9. Episode 2 — THE SECOND SHOT

Objective: determine Sameer’s role and separate the first apparent shooting from the later fatal event.

End belief: Sameer fired the shot, but the family controlled what happened afterwards.

This episode deliberately gives the expert player an early answer, then refuses to let that answer finish the mystery.

Sequence:

1. The player finds evidence of a private shooting/reel at the wedding. Sameer was not merely photographing the celebration; he helped create the setup.
2. Kunal’s footage establishes that Kunal fired first. A second piece of evidence places Sameer with the weapon later.
3. The player reconstructs that the two firing events were separate. The earlier Kunal firing is not the shot that struck Dilip.
4. The player discovers that Sameer fired the later shot that hit Dilip.
5. This seems to solve the mystery. Sameer is the shooter.
6. Then the player finds a condition/update record showing Dilip was still conscious after the shooting.
7. The hospital searches and emergency-call history now acquire a new meaning: the critical question is no longer who fired, but what happened during the period after the injury.
8. The player uncovers the distinction between three kinds of money: Sameer’s legitimate work balance, post-incident leverage, and money connected to the family’s effort to make the incident disappear.
9. Raju confirms that Dilip knew Sameer and expected him to help. This makes Sameer’s relationship with Dilip personal rather than incidental.
10. Nitin’s messages reveal a possible attempt to arrange transport.
11. The player is told or discovers that a vehicle did reach the service entrance, apparently for Dilip.
12. Episode cliffhanger: the vehicle left empty. Someone had told Nitin that Dilip was already on his way to treatment.

Episode 2 key reveal: Sameer fired the shot — but the player has not yet understood what happened after it, or how Sameer’s own decision altered that aftermath.

## 10. Episode 3 — THE CANCELLED RESCUE

Objective: reconstruct the missing interval, understand why the rescue failed, and determine what Sameer wanted the player to conclude.

End belief: Sameer and the family are both responsible, but not in the same way.

Sequence:

1. The player reconstructs the vehicle attempt using multiple sources: Nitin’s account, arrival information, the property layout, and the vehicle’s independent movement.
2. The player establishes that Nitin had started a genuine rescue attempt.
3. The player discovers Sameer’s message/statement telling Nitin that Dilip had already left for treatment.
4. The player establishes that Sameer knew Dilip was still on the property when he said this.
5. This becomes the deepest recontextualization: Sameer was not merely trapped by Bhasin. He actively caused another person to stop trying to help.
6. The player revisits the fire footage. Sameer was not only an observer. He participated in the disposal and retained the footage as counter-evidence.
7. The player discovers that the family had their own evidence of Sameer firing. Sameer’s phone was therefore sent as a counter-file: evidence of the aftermath that he hoped would stop the shooting from becoming the entire story.
8. The player audits what Sameer left on the phone and what he deliberately omitted. His archive is truthful but curated toward a preferred boundary of responsibility.
9. Sameer’s final position becomes clear: he wants the player to prove that the family’s choices after the shooting mattered, while minimizing the significance of the lie that stopped Nitin’s rescue attempt.
10. The player must now decide what to do with a complete but morally uncomfortable picture: Sameer caused the injury, Bhasin controlled the response, Nitin believed a lie, Dilip remained alive during the missing interval, and the body was destroyed afterward.

Episode 3 key reveal: The deepest lie in the phone is not a fabricated event. It is a true sequence arranged to make Sameer’s responsibility appear to end earlier than it actually does.

## 12. Player Investigation Model

The player should build claims, not simply collect clues.

A claim has four parts:

- Observation — what the file literally shows.
- Inference — what the player thinks that means.
- Contradiction — what another source complicates.
- Action — what the player chooses to do based on the current theory.
Example: “Dilip is holding an envelope” is an observation. “Dilip accepted the settlement” is an inference. A later message that the envelope was taken back becomes a contradiction. Choosing to accuse the family before resolving that contradiction becomes the player’s action.

## 13. Investigation Branches

| Route | What it can establish | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Protect Nitin | The attempted rescue, his understanding of the transport, and Sameer’s misleading assurance. | You may lose an early admission from Kunal if Nitin is exposed too soon. |
| Confront Kunal | The sequence of firing, his role in the private shoot, and the family’s internal story. | He may identify Nitin and close that witness route. |
| Work-record route | Sameer’s involvement in arranging the private sequence, payment timing, and professional motive. | Slower, less emotionally direct. |
| Raju route | Dilip’s expectations, his relationship with Sameer, and the human meaning of the missing-person thread. | Aggressive or overconfident handling can make Raju withdraw. |
| Meera route | Preservation, source provenance, and a more disciplined reconstruction. | Less immediate access to personal/emotional context. |
| Sameer route | His interpretation of his own conduct and why he sent the phone. | Giving him too much of your working theory lets him reshape his account. |

## 14. Decision Architecture

Choices should alter the player’s information state, not merely the final dialogue.

| Decision | Immediate effect | Long-term consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Answer Sameer’s mother | She learns an unknown person has the phone. | Changes who starts looking for the device and can accelerate pressure. |
| Reply to Raju early | Raju enters the story before the player understands Dilip. | Early certainty can damage trust if later corrected. |
| Protect Nitin | Preserves his cooperation. | May delay access to Kunal’s strongest self-protective evidence. |
| Expose Nitin | Forces a fast response from Kunal/Bhasin. | Nitin becomes less willing to help later. |
| Publish early theory | Creates immediate public pressure. | A later correction cannot erase the earlier accusation. |
| Preserve evidence first | Builds a stronger factual record. | Costs time and may allow other characters to move first. |
| Give Sameer the “rescuer” narrative | May secure his cooperation. | Preserves his preferred boundary of responsibility. |
| Confront Sameer with the full chain | Tests whether he accepts the false assurance as part of his responsibility. | Can unlock the strongest accountability route, but may end cooperation. |

## 15. Ending Architecture

Three principal endings, with one early-exit route. The endings are based on what the player established and what they did with it.

### Ending A — THE COMPLETE RECORD

- The player establishes the shooting, the post-shooting survival window, the failed rescue attempt, Sameer’s false reassurance, the cover-up, and the way the phone was curated.
- The player preserves source distinctions and does not claim more than the evidence can support.
- The resulting account separates Kunal’s enabling conduct, Sameer’s shooting and lie, Bhasin’s control, Nitin’s complicity and failure to verify, and the final disposal.
- The story resolves without pretending that one person explains everything.

### Ending B — SAMEER’S VERSION

- The player establishes that Sameer fired and that Bhasin controlled the aftermath, but never establishes or chooses to disclose the false reassurance that stopped Nitin.
- The final story is largely factual but preserves Sameer’s preferred boundary: “I caused the accident; they caused the death.”
- The player has not invented evidence. They have given authority to an incomplete interpretation of it.

### Ending C — THE WRONG STORY

- The player commits to Kunal as the killer based on the early firing footage and Sameer’s framing, then acts publicly before separating the two firing events.
- The family produces the later material showing Sameer with the weapon.
- The player’s story collapses because they confused a convincing sequence with a demonstrated causal sequence.

### Early Exit — RETURN TO SENDER

- The player decides the phone is not their responsibility and transfers it through the legitimate channel.
- The Delhi story remains partially unresolved from the player’s perspective.

## 16. The Final Reveal

The final reveal should land in two steps.

First reveal: Sameer fired the shot. This should not be the last reveal. It is the point at which the player realizes the obvious mystery was only the surface.

Final reveal: Sameer sent the phone because he wanted an independent investigator to establish that the family’s actions mattered after the shooting — but he deliberately removed the part of the record that showed how his own false reassurance stopped another person from trying to save Dilip.

The most important line of the story is therefore not “I shot him.” It is effectively:

**“I told him Dilip had already left. I knew he hadn’t.”**

That is the revelation that converts Sameer from a compromised witness into a person actively shaping the causal story the player receives.

## 17. Thematic Spine

The chapter is about responsibility being divided, transferred, and edited.

- Kunal creates the opportunity for the shooting.
- Sameer fires the shot.
- Bhasin controls the response.
- Nitin accepts a false reassurance and abandons an attempted rescue.
- Sameer helps destroy the body.
- The family uses money and reputation to contain the narrative.
- Sameer curates the phone to contain his own share of responsibility.
- The player becomes the final person asked to decide where responsibility belongs.
The chapter’s central question is therefore:

**“When an accident becomes a chain of choices, where does responsibility actually end?”**

## 18. Realism / Plot-Hole Audit

| Risk | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Why does Sameer keep the phone? | A guilty person preserving evidence can look irrational. | He has evidence against the family and fears their selective material will define him; sending the original is a deliberate risk, not a safe move. |
| Why not call police immediately? | The obvious action is emergency reporting. | The story must show pressure, fear, self-interest, and competing responses; emergency access itself is not removed from the world. |
| Why would Nitin believe Sameer? | A rescue cannot depend on one text. | Give Nitin an existing professional hierarchy with Sameer and a plausible reason to believe transport is already being handled. Use corroborating vehicle evidence. |
| Why does Dilip remain on the property? | A victim cannot simply cooperate with obvious danger. | He is injured, dependent on others, believes Sameer is helping, and is not initially shown the full level of danger. |
| Why burn the body? | Burning does not magically erase evidence. | Treat the fire as destructive but imperfect; the main mystery is why they choose it, not magical forensic erasure. |
| Why doesn’t the family destroy every record? | The phone contains too much evidence. | Different people have different devices and records. The conflict is over which version survives and which gets believed. |
| Why is the player investigating? | A random civilian solving a serious crime can feel forced. | Give them a bounded reason to catalogue the phone and a credible route to Meera/professional handling. The player investigates because the evidence contradicts itself. |
| Too many coincidences | A thriller can become “the writer needed this.” | Each major event should have a prior cause: the job, the relationship, the firing, the rescue attempt, the payment, the phone handoff. |
| The drone as omniscient proof | A perfect aerial shot makes the answer too easy. | Use multiple imperfect sources; the player reconstructs the event instead of finding one perfect recording. |
| Too much legal certainty | Game may overstate what a phone proves. | The player establishes a factual reconstruction and uncertainty; formal legal outcomes remain outside the game’s immediate control. |

## 19. Pacing Target

The chapter should feel substantial without filler. A target of roughly 45–70 minutes per episode is appropriate for prototyping, with final duration driven by testing.

Target investigative density: approximately 18 meaningful investigative sequences across the chapter, with 8–12 significant revelations distributed between them.

The player should be able to suspect the shooter early. The challenge is not hiding the answer; the challenge is making the answer insufficient.

Every meaningful sequence should produce at least one: new fact, theory change, contradiction, relationship change, danger increase, or new question.

## 20. Intended Final Player Experience

The chapter should leave the player with these thoughts in sequence:

1. “Who is this guy?”
2. “What happened at that wedding?”
3. “That worker died.”
4. “Sameer saw it.”
5. “Wait — Sameer fired the gun.”
6. “Then why was the boy still alive?”
7. “Someone actually tried to get him out.”
8. “Sameer stopped that attempt.”
9. “So the phone is not a confession. It is Sameer’s version of a confession.”
10. “He gave me the truth — but edited where he wanted responsibility to end.”

The ideal response after finishing is not simply satisfaction at identifying the culprit. It is the urge to reopen earlier evidence and ask whether the player interpreted it correctly.

## 21. Chapter 1 → Chapter 2

Chapter 1 should be fully resolved on its own. Mumbai should not contain the missing answer to the Delhi case.

After the Delhi resolution, Meera receives a separate case involving another handset in Mumbai. The player’s role is now established: someone who can reconstruct a life from a phone without automatically accepting its owner’s version of events.

The sequel hook should promise a new mystery, not tease an unfinished one.

## 22. Consolidated Chapter 1 Story

A stranger’s phone arrives with a simple instruction: the evidence is on it, and someone named M. is supposed to know what to do. The owner is Sameer Khurana, a Delhi wedding photographer on the edge of a career move to Mumbai.

Among hundreds of wedding photographs, the player notices a young waiter who later appears on a missing-person poster. Sameer’s voice notes suggest that the boy was killed at the wedding, that a powerful family concealed what happened, and that the body was burned. The player initially understands Sameer as a frightened witness.

The investigation then cracks that story open. Sameer was not merely covering the wedding. He was helping produce a private reel and had directly handled the gun. The player discovers that there were two separate firing events and that Sameer fired the later shot that struck Dilip.

But that is not the end. Dilip survived. The real mystery becomes the missing period between the shooting and the fire. Sameer searched for medical help. Nitin attempted to arrange transport. A vehicle actually arrived. Yet Dilip never left the property.

The player eventually establishes why: Sameer knowingly told Nitin that Dilip had already gone for treatment. Nitin believed him and stopped his attempt. Bhasin continued to control the response. Dilip died. The body was burned.

The player then understands why the phone exists. Sameer knew the family possessed evidence that showed him firing the gun. He sent his own phone as a counter-file, hoping an independent person would prove that what happened after the shooting mattered too. But Sameer curated the device around a boundary he wanted to preserve: he caused the accident; they caused the death.

The final decision is therefore not simply whether the player can identify the shooter. It is whether the player will repeat Sameer’s preferred version, act on an incomplete but convincing story, or reconstruct and preserve the complete chain of responsibility.

Chapter 1 ends with the player having inherited something more dangerous than a dead man’s phone: another person’s interpretation of the truth.

# Raakh: why people would leave, and the new Episode 3

*2026-10-24. An investor's read of the chapter as it stood, what great endings
do that ours didn't, and the redesign that follows from it. CHAPTER1.md, "The
living witness", is the build record.*

---

## 1. The read, as an investor

**The concept is strong.** A stranger's phone, a real Indian city, apps that
behave exactly like the real ones, and a story the player reconstructs rather
than watches. The first ten minutes (a parcel, 47 missed calls, a brother on
the line, a missing-person poster) grip.

**The delivery loses people in the last third, and that's where retention is
made.** People don't recommend a story for its middle. They recommend it for
how it ended, and they come back for the next chapter because of how the last
one made them *feel* in its final five minutes. Here's what a player felt in
Episode 3 as it stood:

| What Episode 3 asked of the player | What it felt like |
|---|---|
| Four more filings (Q10–Q13) | An audit. The world didn't change; the paperwork did. |
| Sameer's "I knew he hadn't" | A confirmation of what they'd already filed one question earlier. No surprise. |
| Bhasin's call, a man at the gate | Threats narrated *at* the player, then resolved in an epilogue line. Nothing was ever at risk *in play*. |
| Send, post, or return the record | A menu. The outcome was a page of text. |
| Dilip | Already dead before the chapter starts. Nothing the player does can change anything for the person they've come to care about. |

**The emotional peak of the chapter was in Episode 2** ("Sameer." / "Alive.").
Episode 3 de-escalated: a thriller that ends on reconciliation of a ledger.

### Why people would leave unsatisfied
1. **No stakes in the present.** Every event happened a week ago. The only present-tense danger (Bhasin, the gate) never actually forces a decision under pressure.
2. **No agency over what matters.** The player can't save, lose, or change anything for Dilip. Their choices change *wording*.
3. **No final turn.** The last reveal (the lie) is the logical end of a deduction the player has already made, so it lands as "yes, as I thought", not as astonishment.
4. **The ending is read, not played.** The climax is a confirmation dialog.
5. **Nothing to tell a friend.** "I traced 9 of 11 links" is a score, not a story. Word of mouth needs a question: *"Did you get there in time?"*

### Why people wouldn't come back
- No ending made them *need* to know what they'd missed.
- Endings differed in prose, not in fate, so a replay promises more reading, not a different night.
- No final image stays with them, and nothing pulls them toward Chapter 2.

### Smaller drags (fixed or fixed in this pass)
- A newcomer was never told *what they're doing tonight* (fixed: the brief).
- The case file was a manila sheet in a black, night-time game (fixed: dark).
- Too much reading per beat in Hinglish+English; heavy filing (eased: words all on offer, short proof lists, specific feedback).
- Placeholders where pictures and video should be (fixed in this pass).

## 2. What the best endings do (research)

- **Suspense beats surprise, unless the surprise is the point.** Hitchcock to
  Truffaut: show the audience the bomb under the table and they'll grip their
  seats for fifteen minutes; hide it and you get fifteen seconds of shock. The
  exception he names is the twist that *is* the highlight. A great finale uses
  both: a bomb we can see, and a turn we couldn't.
  ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/alfred-hitchcock-and-francois-truffaut-explain-surprise-vs-suspense), [Futility Closet](https://www.futilitycloset.com/2023/12/20/hitchcocks-bomb-theory/))
- **Reversal and recognition, at the same moment.** Aristotle ranks plots
  where the situation flips (*peripeteia*) at the very moment someone learns
  the truth (*anagnorisis*) as the strongest. *Oedipus* is the model.
  ([Wikipedia: Peripeteia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripeteia), [CriticaLink](https://english.hawaii.edu/criticalink/aristotle/gloss/gloss11.html))
- **Surprising, yet inevitable.** A twist works when it's unpredictable going
  forward and obvious looking back: clues planted in plain sight, where the
  audience's attention is elsewhere, worked backwards from the reveal.
  ([Electric Literature](https://electricliterature.com/surprising-yet-inevitable-ending-read-like-a-writer/), [Backstage](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/how-to-write-plot-twist-ideas-8381/))
- ***Searching* hides its ending on screen.** Aneesh Chaganty's screenlife
  thriller spells its twist out in an email inbox the audience scrolls past.
  The genre's own trick: the answer is on the device all along.
  ([/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/searching-easter-egg/))
- ***Gone Girl* turns at the midpoint, from mystery to thriller,** with one
  fact: the missing woman is alive. Everything before is reread through it.
  ([Story Grid](https://storygrid.com/writers-room-gone-girl-middle-build-2/))
- **A clock and an impossible choice.** Thriller craft: a deadline the story
  honours, stakes that are personal not abstract, a protagonist forced to
  choose between bad options under pressure.
  ([Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/10-dos-and-donts-for-writing-page-turning-thriller-novels), [Writers' Store](https://www.writersstore.com/amp-up-the-stakes-create-a-ticking-clock/))
- **In narrative games, the accounting is the reward, and trust is the
  mechanic.** *Return of the Obra Dinn* pays off a full reckoning of every
  fate; *Her Story* lets the player decide how far to trust its narrator.
  ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2019/06/06/return-of-the-obra-dinn-lucas-pope/), [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/road-to-the-igf-sam-barlow-s-i-her-story-i-))

**What this means for Raakh:** keep the reckoning (the chain) and the
unreliable narrator (Sameer), and add the three things it lacked: a bomb the
player can see (a deadline tonight), a reversal-with-recognition that rereads
everything (a fact planted since minute one), and a choice under pressure
whose outcome is a *life*, not a paragraph.

## 3. The redesign: "The living witness"

### The twist
**Dilip is alive.** He did not die on the property. He was unconscious at
2:41; Bhasin's car took him out at 2:55, not to a hospital but to a private
nursing home in Fatehpur Beri owned by a doctor who owes Bhasin, admitted as
"Deepak, 19, road accident". Bhasin rang Sameer at 3:31 and told him the boy
was gone, and that what was left had to be burned. Sameer carried the
jerrycan for a mattress, the sheets and a shirt. **There was never a body.**
Bhasin bound Sameer to the cover-up with a death that hadn't happened.

### Why it's surprising
Everyone, Sameer included, has said "they burned him" since the first voice
note. The game itself has called it a death.

### Why it's inevitable (planted in plain sight)
| From | The plant |
|---|---|
| Episode 1 | The fire clip never shows a body. Raju's poster says *missing*, not dead. |
| Episode 1, Instagram | Under the LAPATA poster on Samastipur Samachar, a comment from @lissy.anita: "Isko kahin dekha hai shayad 🙏". |
| Episode 2 | Dilip's locked chat: "last seen Tuesday 3:12 AM". And Sameer's "Dilip… maaf kar de" from Monday has *two* grey ticks: delivered to a phone that should have burned. |
| Episode 2 | Raju: "Uska phone kabhi kabhi ring jaata hai. Koi uthata nahi." |
| Episode 2, screenshots | Pappu, 2:55: "Sir gaadi nikal gayi." Bhasin: "Theek hai. Baaki back plot pe." |
| Episode 3 | The whole fire clip: a rolled mattress, sheets, a shirt. "Uske kapde bhi." No body. Q12 now asks *what burned*. |
| Minute one | The note: "Local thane mat le jaana. Bhasin ke log wahan baithe hain." It becomes the trap in the final choice. |

### Reversal and recognition, at once
At 2:10 AM on the player's night, **Dilip's own number writes to Sameer's
phone.** It's the nurse, Sister Anita Lissy, on night duty: a boy admitted as
someone else, a gunshot wound called a road accident, who woke last night and
said one name, *Sameer bhaiya*. His belongings: a maroon waistcoat with a
badge, **DILIP**, the badge the player zoomed into in Episode 1. And a
four-second recording of the patient whispering, in the voice the player heard
practise English for Mumbai. **"Alive."** — again, and this time it means
*now*.

The recognition is the player's, and Sameer's too: the man who curated the
phone to end his responsibility before a death was himself curated by Bhasin.
Everybody edited the night.

### The bomb under the table
They're moving him at **7 AM**. The nurse heard it from the man who comes in a
safari suit. The same man who's been asking the player's guard for their flat.

### The choice under pressure
A timed decision, played full screen. Whom do you call first, and second?
- **112 / the local police:** the note warned you. Bhasin's people sit there.
  He's moved within the hour.
- **Meera:** she knows a DCP at Saket who isn't Bhasin's; she needs someone
  who can identify him.
- **Raju:** he'll go now, alone, and be turned away at the gate unless
  someone with authority is with him.
- **Sameer:** the man who sent help away at 1:52 is twenty minutes away. Does
  he go this time? **It depends on how you treated him.** Confronted with the
  truth, he goes, and hands himself in at the door. Given the rescuer story,
  he says he can't, and the minutes go.
- **Post it:** pressure on everyone, and on Bhasin first. He moves him before
  anyone arrives.

**Meera plus Raju, or Meera plus a confronted Sameer, gets there before
seven. Every other first call loses him.**

### The endings, re-cut
The record still matters (who's named, what's proved), but the headline is now
a fate:
- **Found** · "Dilip Kumar Mahto is alive." Raju's photograph at dawn, his
  hand in his brother's. Dilip's first words, a voice note: "Bhaiya… light
  ready hai."
- **Gone by seven** · "Dilip Kumar Mahto is still missing." The nurse's last
  message at 6:58: "Le gaye." A new poster, weeks later.
- Plus the record's own consequences (Complete, Sameer's version, Wrong
  story, Return to sender) underneath.

**The share line becomes a question people answer:** *"Did you get there
before seven?"*

### What it does for retention
- A last fifteen minutes with a live stake, a clock, and a choice that decides
  a life.
- A twist that makes the player go back through the phone ("the ticks were
  *two*"): replay for the rereading, not just the score.
- A failure state that stings, so a second play has a reason.
- An ending image that stays: a boy alive, his hand, his brother.

## 4. Further enhancements (not all in this pass)

- **Chapter-end trailers.** A 10-second teaser for Chapter 2, cut like the reel take.
- **A "Night recap" share card** with the player's choices as a timeline
  ("2:14 called Meera · 2:15 called Raju · 6:41 Dilip found").
- **Friends' outcomes, in the open.** "38% got there before seven" after you finish (behind the existing 50-answer threshold).
- **Voice actors** for the five principals, replacing the synthetic voices.
- **A real-time mode**: the 7 AM clock runs in real minutes for players who want it.
- **Episode drops**: release Episode 3 a day after 1–2 for launch, so the cliffhanger travels on WhatsApp.
- **A 90-second vertical trailer** made from the clips, for Instagram and WhatsApp status.
